Game Design Pitch · June 2026 · Work in Progress

A Ridiculous Game

A traveling crew. A town with three factions. 30 days to build something that makes people feel less alone. Every relationship is a resource. Every day is a choice you can't undo.

Genre Strategy / Social RPG / Installation Art Sim Aesthetic EGA Pixel, Maniac Mansion era Engine Phaser 4 + Bun

Where It Comes From

Captain Tsubasa
Tecmo · Super Famicom · 1992–94
Borrowing: the frozen moment. When a confrontation begins, time stops. Both sides pick a move in secret. Both reveal simultaneously. Sport as a series of dramatic, bluffable duels — not a physics simulation.
Nobunaga's Ambition
Koei · SNES · 1993
Borrowing: place as organism. Each territory has loyalty, development, morale. Neglect one province while fighting another and it collapses. Named officers who age, defect, die. Soft power before violence.
Persona 3
Atlus · PlayStation 2 · 2006
Borrowing: relationships as the real game. Social bonds directly power your ability to act. A calendar that makes every day irreversible. One action before bed. The dungeon is the payoff — the day is the game.

All three games share one idea Western design missed: combat is the surface layer, not the core. The action is the payoff for investment in a deeper systemic layer. This game applies that idea to something real — building public art in a specific place, with specific people, in limited time.

The World

The Circuit

A small crew that travels from town to town building connection infrastructure — temporary public installations, light works, experience design. They arrive with a van, a plan, and 30 days. Then they leave. What they built stays.

Inspired by real companies like teamLab, Moment Factory, and the work of Rafael Lozano-Hemmer. The "installation" is a LED work on the face of a public building. It responds to the community. It stores collective memory.

The Town

Every town has the same three forces in different proportions:

The Council — controls money and permits. Transactional. Wants ROI.
The Collective — controls space and people. Ideological. Wants ownership.
The Elders — controls meaning and memory. Cultural. Wants respect, not just acknowledgment.

None of them fully trust each other. None of them fully trust you.

How It Works — Three Layers

The Confrontation

Layer 1 ← Captain Tsubasa

When you enter a significant meeting, negotiation, or stand-off, time freezes. The screen shifts to a close-up. Both you and your opponent secretly choose a posture: Challenge, Yield, Redirect, or Hold. Both are revealed simultaneously. The outcome depends on the combination — not on a stat check, but on reading the other person correctly.

Each character has a Guts meter. Your best moves cost Guts. Run low and your options thin. This is the "what do you want to do?" moment from the soccer game Jon played as a kid. The drama is real because the opponent's choice is hidden until the reveal.

Example: The Council chair is about to reject your permit. You choose Redirect — pivot to the economic angle. He chose Challenge — expected you to defend the art. Your Redirect lands. He's off-footing. You gain a concession.

The Territory

Layer 2 ← Nobunaga's Ambition

The town is divided into zones — each with its own stats: Trust, Access, and Resonance. Trust drops if you ignore a faction. Access closes if Trust falls too low. Resonance measures how much the community has internalized what you're building — high Resonance means the installation means something here; low Resonance means it's just a screen.

Each crew member you assign to a zone affects it differently. Hana's espionage reveals hidden agendas. Gorou's field presence boosts Access. Miko's relationship work builds Trust. You can't be everywhere. You choose where to invest and what to let slide.

Example: You've been neglecting the Collective to focus on the Council. Their Trust drops to 30. They start a counter-narrative on local social media. The Council hears about it. Your Access to the building site falls. You didn't fight anyone — you just didn't show up.

The Relationship

Layer 3 ← Persona 3

Each character on your crew has a Social Link — a story arc across up to 10 ranked encounters. Leveling a Social Link unlocks new confrontation options, zone-specific bonuses, and alternate endings. Neglecting a character long enough causes them to drift — and Daichi will defect entirely.

Social Links also connect to townspeople. The Elders' liaison, the Collective's organizer, the Council's deputy — each is a character with their own arc. Building those bonds changes what's possible in Layer 1 and Layer 2. Relationships are the primary progression system.

Example: You've built Miko's Social Link to Rank 6. She can now read the Collective organizer's tells before a Confrontation starts — you see a hint of her likely choice. It's not a guaranteed win. It's an edge.

The Calendar

You have exactly 30 days. Each day has a morning slot and an evening slot. You choose one thing per slot. Some things are time-locked — miss the window and it's gone. The installation date is fixed. The calendar fills up faster than you expect.

A Day in the Game

Morning · Territory

Check the Zone Report

Trust levels updated overnight. The Collective dropped 8 points — someone talked. You decide where to send Hana.

Morning Slot · Relationship

Coffee with the Elders' Liaison

Miko's Social Link Rank 4. You learn the Elders have a grievance that predates you. This shapes Day 18's confrontation.

Afternoon · Confrontation

Emergency Council Meeting

Permit review triggered by the Collective's social media push. Frozen moment. You choose Redirect. The chair chose Challenge. It lands — barely. Guts cost: 20.

Afternoon Slot · Territory

Gorou on-site at the building

Access +12. He's the best at this. You note the bar on his clock moved again.

Evening · Relationship

One action before bed

Train Ren's Confidence, spend time with Daichi (loyalty at 42 — borderline), or rest to recover Guts. You can't do all three. Day 12 of 30.

Still Open

  • Does the game move between multiple towns (campaign) or go deep on one town (single arc)?
  • How much do player choices change the installation's final form? Is the ending visual, narrative, or both?
  • Is there a failure state, or does every run produce an ending that reflects what you chose?
  • What does the confrontation screen look like — is it full-screen drama, or subtle?
  • Should the Elders layer involve a cultural advisory mechanic (parallel to real Hearthwell process)?
  • Multiplayer? (Two players as co-directors — each controlling different crew members.)
