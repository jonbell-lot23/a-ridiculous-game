## Session 1: Origin Story

**Format:** Voice memos → transcribed → Claude session

### The Idea

Jon was playing Super Famicom games at his friend Takiki's house as a kid. All in Japanese, couldn't read them. Two games stuck:

1. A soccer game where mid-match challenges paused the game and showed an anime-style confrontation — multiple choice, dramatic pose, sound effect, play resumes. Very "what do you want to do?"

2. A feudal Japan strategy game — SimCity meets Civilization, broken into territory sections. Mind-blowing because nothing like it existed in the US market.

The question: what if vibe coding could blend several of these Super Famicom games together? Research different games, figure out what made each great, find an art style, blend it.

Skepticism: most of a game is sound design and human tuning. Vibe coding alone probably can't make something good. But with sustained effort over months, maybe.

The broader idea: in a world without creative team limitations, you could combine 10 of your favorite games. Maybe whole new genres get invented.

---

### Research Session

**Identified the games:**

**Captain Tsubasa** (Tecmo, Super Famicom III/IV/V, 1992-94)
- Never officially localized
- "Cinematic Soccer" — Tecmo's own term
- Core mechanic: pause-and-choose confrontation with *hidden simultaneous choice*. Both players pick simultaneously, reveal together.
- Guts economy: special moves cost a scarce resource. Can't spam your best player's Drive Shot. Attrition + timing puzzle layered over the bluffing system.
- Matches are manga chapters — scripted story events, not simulations. Win/loss conditions tied to narrative beats.
- Goalkeeper duel = separate rock-paper-scissors minigame with stat differentials.
- The gap: this specific pause-and-choose mechanic with simultaneous hidden choice has never been revived in a standalone modern game for any sport.

**Nobunaga's Ambition** (Koei, SNES 1993/94)
- Province-as-organism model: each territory has loyalty, development level, military readiness, population. Neglect loyalty → peasant revolt. Over-tax to fund a war elsewhere → destabilization cascade.
- Officer/vassal layer on top: named historical figures with differentiated stats (Intelligence, War Ability, Leadership, Loyalty, Age). They age, defect, die, get assassinated.
- Pre-combat soft power: espionage, assassination, crop destruction, rumor spreading, bribery — all before you move armies.
- Two-tier structure: strategic province map + separate tactical battle grid.
- Western players had never seen this on a console. Civilization was PC-only and barely known.

**Persona lineage** (Atlus)
- Proto-Persona = SMT if... (Super Famicom, 1994). High school setting. Guardian system (death transforms you rather than ending you). This was the direct basis for Persona.
- Full structure crystallized in Persona 3 (2006): day/night calendar, school life, one action per time slot, Social Links powering combat.
- The critical mechanical insight: Social Links *directly* power dungeon combat. Relationships aren't filler — they're the primary progression system.
- Calendar = irreversible time pressure. Exactly one school year. Specific days matter. Missing a window is permanent. Creates genuine attachment and genuine regret.
- Negotiation system (talk to enemies to recruit/collect them) has never been fully committed to in any modern Persona-like. Open niche.

---

### The Synthesis

All three games share one philosophy:

> Combat is the *surface layer*, not the core. The action is the payoff for investment in a deeper systemic layer.

Western design of the same era inverted this — action was primary, everything else was filler.

**The gap that doesn't exist in any current game:**
- Pause-and-choose confrontations with hidden simultaneous choice (Captain Tsubasa)
- Territory management where each zone has its own health stats (Nobunaga)
- Social bonds that mechanically power both of the above (Persona)
- A calendar making every choice irreversible (Persona)
- Named characters who can age, defect, or die permanently (both)
